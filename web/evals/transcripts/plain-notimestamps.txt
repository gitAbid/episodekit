Welcome to Second Draft, the podcast about rewriting. Today's episode is about the first ten minutes of a piece of writing, because that's where most drafts die, and where almost all repairs are cheap if you catch them early.

Here is the pattern I see in my own drafts and in every manuscript I've edited. The opening tries to earn attention with background. The writer warms up on the page. Chapter one is the pitcher in the bullpen; the game starts in chapter two. Readers can feel the difference, even when they can't name it, and they leave.

The repair is mechanical, which is the good news. Find the first concrete scene, the first number, the first moment where two people want different things. Cut everything above it. Paste the cut text into a notes file, because your ego needs to see that nothing is lost. Then rebuild the smallest bridge you can from that scene backward to whatever context the reader truly cannot live without.

Two warnings from the field. First, this cut is harder on nonfiction than fiction, because nonfiction writers believe their context is the argument. It usually isn't; the argument is one sentence, and you'll find it hiding in the third paragraph. Second, do this repair on paper if you can. Screens forgive; paper keeps you honest about how long the bridge actually is.

A practical drill for this week. Take something you wrote and love. Photocopy the first three pages. With scissors, literally scissors, cut at the first concrete thing. Read the new opening aloud. If you feel a small click, that's the draft starting where the story starts. Everything before the click was rehearsal.

Next week: endings, and why the last sentence of a piece should cost you something to write. Until then, keep your drafts ugly and your scissors sharp.
