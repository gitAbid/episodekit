[00:00] Narrator: The Lighthouse Keeper's Log, part four. March, 1897. The keeper of Egg Rock light writes in his ledger every night at nine, and the ledger says the same thing most nights: all well. Tonight the ledger is wrong, and he knows it as he writes it.
[00:41] Narrator: The supply boat came Thursday with flour, oil, and a newspaper three weeks old. The newspaper mentions a storm that never arrived here, and the keeper has learned to read the paper the way sailors read clouds, for news of what's coming rather than what has happened.
[01:26] Narrator: Friday the glass begins to fall. Not quickly, the way it does in stories, but patiently, a tenth of an inch an hour, as if the weather were counting something out. He checks the lamp, trims the wicks, fills the reservoir, and winds the clockwork that turns the lens, eight turns to the inch of fall.
[02:19] Narrator: By Saturday noon the sea has changed its mind about the shore. Waves take the landing in flat gray sheets, and the keeper counts the intervals between them out of old habit. Seven seconds. Then six. When the interval stops shrinking, he tells himself, the storm has done its worst. He has said this to himself across nineteen winters, and it has never once been evidence.
[03:17] Narrator: In the night the lamp turns and turns. Somewhere south of the rock a fishing schooner runs for harbor with her crew swearing at the light they cannot see, and the keeper stands his watch, awake for no reason he could defend in an admiralty court, and the ledger in the tower room still says, in ink now dry, all well.
[04:21] Narrator: Sunday breaks the storm's back at dawn. The sea remembers being a lake. The keeper walks the gallery, counts shingles lost, finds the boat intact and his nerve likewise, more or less. At nine he opens the ledger, writes the wind, the glass, the damage, and then, because he is honest and the column is narrow, all well. The Lighthouse Keeper's Log continues next week.
