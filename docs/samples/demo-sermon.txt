Grace and peace to you, church. If you have your Bibles, open them to the story of
the Prodigal Son — Luke chapter fifteen — because today I want to hold that story up
to the light and let it examine us for a few minutes.

Every one of us knows what it is to wander. The younger son in the story takes his
inheritance early, walks out of his father's house, and spends everything he has on
a far country. And I want you to notice something about that far country: it did
not start far away. It started as one small step, one reasonable decision, one "I
know better than my father." Nobody wakes up in a pigsty; you get there one
step at a time. That is how wandering works, and it is still how it works in your
life and mine.

But the story does not stay in the far country, because the son comes to himself.
He remembers that even the hired servants in his father's house have bread enough
and to spare. Remember that repentance in this story begins not with shame but with
memory — he remembers whose house he came from. Some of you have been running so
long that you have forgotten whose house you came from, and the Father's first
mercy to you today may simply be the memory of home.

Now watch what happens when he turns around, because this is the heart of the
story. While he was still a long way off, his father saw him and ran. In that
culture an old man did not run; running was beneath his dignity. But love has never
been terribly concerned with dignity. The father runs, the father embraces him
before the boy can finish his rehearsed apology, and the robe and the ring and the
shoes come out before the confession is even complete. Grace, church, is not a
wage you negotiate on the porch; it is an embrace you meet on the road.

And then there is the older brother, and this is where the story gets
uncomfortable, because most of us in this room are the older brother. He never
left. He served. He obeyed. And when the music starts, he stands outside the party
and refuses to go in. Hear his complaint: "All these years I have served you, and
you never gave me a young goat." He has been keeping score in a house built on
grace. This is the quiet danger of religious people: you can live in the father's
house and still have the far country in your heart, because resentment can carry
you as far from home as recklessness can.

So what does the father say to the older son? Not a rebuke. "Son, you are always
with me, and everything I have is yours." The father goes out to the angry son the
same way he ran to the returning one. God's mercy is not exhausted by the sinner
who comes back; there is enough left over for the saint who is bitter. Maybe you
came in here this morning angry at someone whose forgiveness was celebrated while
your faithfulness went unnoticed. The father is at the door asking you to come
inside too.

So here is what I am asking you to do this week. First, name your far country.
Stop pretending the wandering is just a season; call it what it is, and turn
around. Second, stop keeping score. The scoreboard is what kept the older brother
outside, and it will keep you outside too. Third, come to the table. Communion is
not a reward for the sorted-out; it is bread for the hungry. If you are hungry,
the table is for you.

Because here is the truth I want you to carry out of this room: the Father is not
waiting to lecture you when you come home. He is waiting to run. The question the
story leaves us with is not whether God will forgive — the story settled that on
the road. The question is whether we will let Him, and whether we will let Him
forgive the brother we are still angry at too.

Let us pray. Father, we have all been in the far country, and some of us are
standing outside the party. Give us the memory of home, the humility to turn
around, and the courage to come to the table. In Jesus' name, amen.
